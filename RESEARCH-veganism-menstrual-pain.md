# Research memo: plant-based eating and menstrual pain

Internal research memo, not the blog post. Written for the author's own evaluation.
Includes more hedging and technical detail than the article should carry. Flags
follow VOICE.md evidence rules: name the study, state sample size and population,
distinguish "shown to" from "may" from "hypothesis," and call out small, old,
unreplicated, or confounded findings explicitly.

---

## 1. The Barnard et al. trial on low-fat vegetarian diet and dysmenorrhea

**Citation:** Barnard ND, Scialli AR, Hurlock D, Bertron P. "Diet and sex-hormone
binding globulin, dysmenorrhea, and premenstrual symptoms." *Obstetrics &
Gynecology.* 2000;95(2):245-250.
Link: https://journals.lww.com/greenjournal/abstract/2000/02000/diet_and_sex_hormone_binding_globulin,.15.aspx
(Abstract also at ScienceDirect: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0029784499005256)

**Design:** Crossover trial. 33 women each served as their own control. Phase one:
low-fat vegetarian diet (no animal products, minimal added fat) for two full
menstrual cycles. Phase two: usual diet plus a daily placebo pill, for two more
cycles. Order was not randomized across all subjects in the way a parallel RCT
would be; this is a single-arm crossover with each subject completing both phases.

**Sample size and duration:** 33 women enrolled; 12 did not complete the study
(reasons included pregnancy, menopause, and noncompliance), which is a large
dropout rate relative to the starting sample. Each phase lasted two menstrual
cycles, so total participation was roughly 4 cycles (about 3-4 months) per
completer.

**What was measured:** Serum sex-hormone binding globulin (SHBG), body weight,
dietary intake, duration and intensity of dysmenorrhea pain, and duration of
premenstrual symptoms, self-reported via diary/questionnaire during each phase.

**What was found:** Mean SHBG was higher during the diet phase (46.7 ± 23.6
nmol/L) than the supplement/placebo phase (39.3 ± 19.8 nmol/L). The diet phase
was also associated with reduced body weight, and with reductions in
dysmenorrhea duration and intensity and in premenstrual symptom duration.

**Flags:**
- **Small and old.** n=33 enrolled, ~21 completers, published in 2000. This is a
  small trial by any standard, and it is now 25 years old.
- **Confounded by weight loss.** Body weight dropped during the diet phase
  alongside the symptom improvements. Weight loss itself is independently
  associated with reduced dysmenorrhea and altered estrogen dynamics, so the
  trial cannot cleanly separate "vegetarian diet" from "ate less fat, lost
  weight."
- **No blinding, and a support-group confound.** Subjects could not be blinded
  to which diet they were eating. The paper itself notes that group support and
  a placebo/expectancy effect from active participation could not be ruled out.
- **Author conflict of interest.** Lead author Neal Barnard is the founder and
  president of the Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine (PCRM), an
  advocacy nonprofit that promotes vegan diets. PCRM's own site currently
  publishes this study under headlines like "Eating Meat Increases Menstrual
  Pain" (https://www.pcrm.org/news/health-nutrition/eating-meat-increases-menstrual-pain)
  and "Vegan Diets Benefit Women's Reproductive Health"
  (https://www.pcrm.org/news/health-nutrition/vegan-diets-benefit-womens-reproductive-health).
  That does not make the data wrong, but it is a real conflict of interest to
  disclose, and it explains why this single small trial gets cited so
  disproportionately in vegan advocacy content relative to its size.
- **Not independently replicated as designed.** I did not find an independent
  group repeating this same low-fat vegetarian vs. usual-diet crossover design
  with SHBG as an endpoint. What exists instead (see below) are looser,
  differently-designed "diet therapy" trials that test broader dietary advice
  (less fat, sugar, salt, caffeine) rather than a vegetarian diet specifically,
  and they are not calling themselves replications of Barnard.

**Closest thing to a follow-up (not a true replication):**
Aydin Kartal Y, Yilmaz Akyuz E. "The effect of diet on primary dysmenorrhea in
university students: a randomized controlled clinical trial." *Pak J Med Sci.*
2018. Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6290229/
- Randomized, 67 female students (34 diet, 33 control), 3-month intervention.
- The "diet" here was general dietary counseling to reduce saturated fat, salt,
  sugar, and alcohol, not a vegetarian/vegan diet. It is testing an overlapping
  but distinct hypothesis (macronutrient/processed-food quality, not plant vs.
  animal food).
- Pain intensity (VAS) fell more in the diet group (7.14 to 5.15) than control
  (7.09 to 6.74), a statistically significant between-group difference.
- Flag: not blinded, self-reported dietary adherence, single-country
  convenience sample of university students, and again no separation of
  dietary composition from any accompanying weight or lifestyle change.

## 2. Dietary fat, SHBG, and circulating estrogen

The mechanistic claim in Barnard's paper: less dietary fat → higher SHBG → less
free/bioactive estrogen → less endometrial prostaglandin substrate → less pain.
Each link has some support, but the overall chain is not consistently
demonstrated end to end, and the fat-SHBG link itself is contested.

**Supporting evidence (older, smaller trials):**
- Goldin BR et al., "Effect of diet on excretion of estrogens in pre- and
  postmenopausal women." *Cancer Res.* Mentioned in review literature on
  dietary lipids as an additional regulator of SHBG:
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3558725/ (Dietary lipids: an additional
  regulator of plasma levels of sex hormone binding globulin) — an early,
  small mechanistic study, not a large RCT.
- Canadian Diet and Breast Cancer Prevention Study Group. "Effects of a low-fat
  high-carbohydrate diet on plasma sex hormones in premenopausal women:
  results from a randomized controlled trial."
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2223807/
  This is a genuine RCT, larger and longer than Barnard's (2-year intervention
  reducing fat to ~15% of calories in premenopausal women), and it did find
  estradiol about 20% lower and progesterone about 35% lower in the
  intervention group. This is the best piece of RCT evidence that a sustained,
  large fat reduction can shift circulating estrogen. It was designed around
  breast cancer risk, not dysmenorrhea, and did not report SHBG-to-pain
  outcomes.

**Best current synthesis, and it is a null-to-mixed result:**
Soltani et al. "The Effect of Low-Fat Diets Versus High-Fat Diet on Sex
Hormones: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled
Trials." *Journal of Food Science.* 2025.
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40387562/ (full text:
https://ift.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1750-3841.70266)
- Pooled 11 RCTs, 888 participants total, comparing low-fat (≤30% of energy)
  vs. higher-fat diets.
- **Found no significant pooled differences in estradiol, estrone, or SHBG**
  between low-fat and high-fat diet groups, despite individual trials (like
  the Canadian study above) showing effects in one direction.
- This is the most important corrective to the fat-SHBG-estrogen story: at the
  meta-analytic level, across mixed populations (not all premenopausal, not
  all women), the effect does not hold up as a reliable, generalizable finding.

**A large observational study that also complicates the SHBG story:**
Key TJ et al. "Oestradiol and sex hormone-binding globulin in premenopausal and
post-menopausal meat-eaters, vegetarians and vegans." *Br J Cancer* or related
EPIC-Oxford-linked analysis. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10424753/
- Cross-sectional comparison: 640 premenopausal women (153 meat-eaters, 382
  vegetarians, 105 vegans) and 457 postmenopausal women (223/196/38 by same
  groups). Roughly 1,100 women total, far larger than Barnard's 33.
- Before adjusting for BMI, vegetarians and vegans had slightly higher SHBG and
  lower estradiol than meat-eaters, in the expected direction.
- **After adjusting for BMI, these differences were essentially eliminated.**
  The authors' interpretation: vegetarians/vegans tend to have lower BMI, and
  it is the lower BMI, not the diet composition per se, that is doing the
  work on SHBG/estradiol.
- Flag: this directly undercuts the idea that avoiding animal products changes
  estrogen exposure through diet composition alone, independent of body
  weight, and is a much larger and more statistically powered dataset than
  Barnard's trial.

**Bottom line for this section:** the fat-SHBG-estrogen chain has genuine
mechanistic plausibility and some old, small positive trials, but the largest
meta-analysis (2025, 11 RCTs) found no significant pooled effect on SHBG or
estrogen, and the largest observational comparison (~1,100 women) found the
apparent diet effect disappeared after controlling for body weight. Any claim
that "a plant-based diet raises SHBG and lowers estrogen" should be stated as
contested, not established, and weight/BMI is a live rival explanation.

## 3. The prostaglandin mechanism in menstrual pain

**Established physiology (strong, mechanistic, and clinically validated):**
- Endometrial tissue synthesizes prostaglandins, chiefly PGF2α and PGE2, during
  the luteal-to-menstrual transition, driven by falling progesterone at the end
  of the cycle.
- In women with primary dysmenorrhea, menstrual endometrium and menstrual fluid
  show markedly higher PGF2α than in women without dysmenorrhea; one classic
  study found endometrial PGF2α concentration on day one of bleeding was
  roughly 4 times higher in dysmenorrheic women than in controls.
  Source: "Endogenous levels of prostaglandin F2α and its main metabolites in
  plasma and endometrium of normal and dysmenorrheic women," *Am J Obstet
  Gynecol* 1978. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/637076/
- PGF2α causes myometrial (uterine muscle) contraction; excessive or prolonged
  contraction causes uterine ischemia, which is the proposed pain mechanism.
- This mechanism is corroborated by treatment response: NSAIDs, which inhibit
  cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and thereby suppress prostaglandin synthesis,
  are an effective, first-line, well-established treatment for primary
  dysmenorrhea. That NSAIDs work by blocking prostaglandin synthesis, and that
  this relieves pain, is about as close to "established mechanism" as this
  field gets.
- 17β-estradiol has been shown to increase prostaglandin F secretion by
  endometrial tissue in vitro, giving a plausible (and reasonably well
  supported) link between estrogen exposure and prostaglandin output.

**Where established mechanism ends and diet hypothesis begins:**
- Prostaglandins are synthesized from arachidonic acid, an omega-6 fatty acid.
  The hypothesis (not established fact) is that dietary fatty acid balance,
  specifically the ratio of omega-6 to omega-3 intake, or total fat/arachidonic
  acid intake, changes how much precursor is available for prostaglandin
  synthesis, and therefore how much PGF2α/PGE2 gets made during menstruation.
- This is biochemically plausible: omega-3 fats (EPA/DHA) compete with
  arachidonic acid for the same enzymes and can shift eicosanoid production
  toward less inflammatory prostaglandin/leukotriene series. But I did not find
  a study that directly measured endometrial or menstrual-fluid prostaglandin
  levels before and after a dietary fat intervention in humans and showed the
  hypothesized shift. The omega-3 RCTs below measure pain outcomes, not
  prostaglandin levels, so the mechanism is being inferred, not demonstrated,
  in the diet-and-pain trials.
- **State this plainly in any writing:** "prostaglandins drive menstrual cramping,
  and NSAIDs work by blocking them" is established. "Diet changes how much
  prostaglandin you make" is a plausible, biochemically reasonable hypothesis,
  not something directly shown in humans with diet-and-tissue-level
  measurement together.

## 4. Dietary fiber and estrogen excretion

**Proposed mechanism:** Estrogen is conjugated in the liver and excreted into
bile, then partially reabsorbed in the gut (enterohepatic circulation). The
hypothesis is that fiber binds estrogen in the colon and/or lowers bacterial
beta-glucuronidase activity (an enzyme that deconjugates estrogen and allows
reabsorption), so more estrogen leaves in stool rather than being recycled,
lowering circulating levels.

**Supporting sources:**
- Rose DP et al. "Effect of dietary components, including lignans and
  phytoestrogens, on enterohepatic circulation and liver metabolism of
  estrogens and on sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG)." Mentioned across
  review literature: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2826899/
- Descriptive review language (fecal steroid/estrogen excretion, bile acid
  literature): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8529021/ and
  purdue dissertation on fiber/fecal steroid excretion:
  https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI9313998
- A related finding: faster intestinal transit (which high fiber intake
  promotes) has been associated with lower serum estrogen in some studies:
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2224051/

**Flag on all of the above:** these are mostly older studies (1980s-90s),
several are reviews summarizing earlier primary work rather than new primary
data, and effect sizes and consistency across fiber types are not uniform. The
review language itself says results are inconsistent by fiber type and "not
all studies found an association."

**Direct contradiction, and a better-powered study:**
Oh H, Smith-Warner SA, Tamimi RM, et al. "Dietary Fat and Fiber Intakes Are Not
Associated with Patterns of Urinary Estrogen Metabolites in Premenopausal
Women." *J Nutr.* 2015;145(9):2109-2116.
Link: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622088666
(PMCID PMC4548163)
- 598 premenopausal women from the Nurses' Health Study II (109 from a
  reproducibility substudy, 489 from a nested case-control study).
- Cross-sectional design; diet assessed by food-frequency questionnaire,
  estrogen metabolites measured directly in mid-luteal urine by
  HPLC-tandem mass spectrometry (a more granular, more rigorous exposure
  measurement than most older studies, which used serum or indirect
  measures).
- **Overall finding: fiber and fat intake were not strongly associated with
  the overall pattern of estrogen metabolism.** Some individual metabolites
  did show scattered associations (higher fiber linked to higher
  4-methoxyestradiol and lower 17-epiestriol; PUFA and trans fat linked to
  17-epiestriol), but the authors' own conclusion was that estrogen metabolism
  "is not a major mechanism" connecting dietary fiber/fat to hormone-related
  disease risk.
- This is a meaningfully larger, more methodologically careful study than the
  older fiber-estrogen literature, and it points the other way. It measured
  urinary metabolites rather than serum estrogen directly, so it is not a
  perfect head-to-head contradiction, but it is the strongest available
  caution against treating "fiber lowers estrogen" as settled.

**Bottom line:** fiber-binds-estrogen is a real, textbook-taught mechanism at
the level of gut physiology (fiber and bile acid binding is well documented),
but whether it meaningfully changes circulating/urinary estrogen levels in
free-living women eating normal diets is not consistently supported, and the
best-measured recent study found no strong association.

## 5. Omega-3 intake and dysmenorrhea: controlled trials

**Systematic review and meta-analysis:**
Mohammadi MM, Mirjalili R, Faraji A. "The impact of omega-3 polyunsaturated
fatty acids on primary dysmenorrhea: a systematic review and meta-analysis of
randomized controlled trials." *Eur J Clin Pharmacol.* 2022;78(5):721-731.
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35059756/
- Pooled RCTs of omega-3 supplementation (fish oil / EPA-DHA capsules, not
  whole-food omega-3 intake) vs. placebo for primary dysmenorrhea pain.
- Result: standardized mean difference of -1.075 (95% CI -1.871 to -0.279)
  favoring omega-3 for reducing pain severity — a statistically significant
  effect, but note the very wide confidence interval, which signals
  meaningful heterogeneity or a small evidence base underneath the pooled
  number.
- Meta-regression suggested the effect was strongest at lower doses and
  weakened with age; the authors themselves characterized the overall effect
  as "mild."
- Flag: abstract does not report the pooled number of trials or total
  participants plainly, which itself signals a modest evidence base typical
  of this literature (most primary dysmenorrhea nutrition RCTs run 40-120
  participants). Treat the effect as "shown in a meta-analysis of a small
  number of small trials," not as a large, well-powered demonstration.

**Newer individual RCT:**
"Effect of Exercise and Omega-3 Supplements on the Quality of Life of Young
Female Patients With Primary Dysmenorrhea: A Randomized Controlled Trial."
2025. Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12357747/
- 72 women aged 18-25, randomized to exercise, omega-3, both, or neither
  (implied factorial-style design), 12-week intervention.
- Pain (VAS) fell significantly in all active groups; the combined
  exercise-plus-omega-3 group improved most.
- Flag: small trial (72 total, so under 20 per arm if four arms), single
  country, self-reported pain outcome, and it tests omega-3 supplements
  (not diet-derived omega-3, and not a vegan-vs-omniverous comparison).

**Relevance to a plant-based-diet framing:** the omega-3 RCTs are testing
supplementation (typically fish oil), not a vegan or vegetarian diet. A
plant-based diet does not automatically raise omega-3 intake and, if it
increases omega-6-rich vegetable oil intake without a corresponding ALA or
algae-based EPA/DHA source, could push the omega-6:omega-3 ratio the wrong
way. This is worth flagging directly if the eventual post implies
"plant-based" and "omega-3-adequate" are the same thing. They are not; a
plant-based diet can be low in EPA/DHA unless algae oil or ample flax/chia/
walnut/ALA sources are deliberately included.

## 6. Well-designed studies finding no effect, or contradicting the above

Collected in one place, since VOICE.md asks to look for these deliberately
rather than only reporting confirming evidence.

1. **Soltani et al. 2025 meta-analysis (11 RCTs, n=888):** no significant
   pooled difference in estradiol, estrone, or SHBG between low-fat and
   high-fat diets. Directly undercuts the fat-SHBG mechanism at the
   meta-analytic level. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40387562/

2. **Key et al., EPIC-Oxford-linked cross-sectional study (~1,100 women):**
   apparent SHBG/estradiol differences between vegetarians/vegans and
   meat-eaters disappeared after adjusting for BMI. Suggests body weight, not
   diet composition, explains most of the hormonal difference sometimes
   attributed to plant-based eating. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10424753/

3. **Oh et al. 2015, Nurses' Health Study II (n=598):** fiber and fat intake
   not strongly associated with overall urinary estrogen metabolite pattern
   in premenopausal women, using a more rigorous exposure measurement than
   most of the older fiber-estrogen literature.
   https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622088666

4. **Barr, 1999, "Vegetarianism and menstrual cycle disturbances: is there an
   association?"** *Am J Clin Nutr.* 1999;70(3 Suppl):549S-554S.
   https://academic.oup.com/ajcn/article/70/3/549S/4715003 and
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10479230/
   This is a review, not a single trial, but it directly addresses the
   question and reports that while early athlete-focused studies (e.g., a
   1984 study of runners) found more vegetarians among amenorrheic athletes,
   a carefully controlled prospective study found vegetarian women did **not**
   have an increased prevalence of subclinical menstrual disturbances. Barr's
   own conclusion is that the vegetarianism-menstrual-disturbance link from
   the athlete literature is confounded by energy availability and dieting
   behavior, not diet composition alone. Directly relevant to area 7 below,
   and also a caution against over-reading the athlete studies.

5. **Barnard's own trial, re-read critically:** the paper's own limitations
   section flags the weight-loss confound and the inability to rule out
   support-group/placebo effects (see area 1). Worth stating as a contradiction
   in the sense that the trial's own design cannot support the causal claim
   most commonly made from it.

**What I did not find:** I did not find a large (200+), well-blinded (as far
as diet trials allow), placebo-controlled RCT that specifically tested a
vegan or vegetarian diet (not just "less fat" or "less saturated fat") against
an omnivorous diet for dysmenorrhea and found a null result. The null and
contradictory evidence that exists is mostly at the mechanism level (SHBG,
estrogen, fiber) rather than at the level of a head-to-head vegan-diet-vs-pain
trial. That absence is itself worth naming: nobody has run the big, clean
version of Barnard's trial, in either direction.

## 7. Menstrual downsides associated with plant-based eating

**Iron status:** this is the best-supported downside, and it applies
specifically to menstruating women because of blood loss.
- Menstruating women lose roughly 10-42 mg of iron per cycle on top of
  baseline losses, and non-heme iron (the only form present in plant foods)
  has substantially lower bioavailability than heme iron from meat.
- A recent review: "Plant-Based Diet and Risk of Iron-deficiency Anemia. A
  Review of the Current Evidence and Implications for Preventive Strategies."
  *Curr Nutr Rep.* 2025. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12174276/
- A Swedish study on teenage girls found iron deficiency in roughly 70% of
  vegans and vegetarians studied, though notably actual anemia rates were
  closer (about 13% in those avoiding red meat vs. 8% in vegans), suggesting
  low iron stores are far more common than frank anemia.
  https://theconversation.com/low-iron-is-common-in-teenage-girls-with-vegans-and-vegetarians-at-greatest-risk-according-to-our-research-in-sweden-253878
- Flag: risk is real and worth naming plainly, but "well-planned" plant-based
  diets (with attention to iron-rich legumes/greens plus vitamin C for
  absorption, and screening) substantially mitigate it. This is a nutrition
  planning issue, not an inherent property of eliminating animal products.

**Cycle irregularity / amenorrhea:** more complicated, and largely confounded
by energy intake rather than diet composition per se.
- Older athlete-focused studies found higher rates of amenorrhea among
  vegetarian athletes (one figure cited: 31% amenorrhea among vegetarian
  athletes vs. 14% on a high-carb/low-fat diet vs. 4% on a "balanced four food
  group" diet), but these studies mix vegetarianism with high training load
  and often with low overall energy intake.
- Barr's 1999 review (cited in area 6) concludes that carefully controlled
  studies do not show an independent effect of vegetarianism itself on
  subclinical menstrual disturbance, once energy availability is accounted
  for.
- The mechanism that is well established is low energy availability, not
  plant-based eating per se: insufficient energy intake relative to
  expenditure suppresses GnRH pulsatility, which lowers LH/FSH, which lowers
  estradiol/progesterone and can cause anovulation or functional hypothalamic
  amenorrhea. This is the same mechanism seen in the Female Athlete Triad /
  RED-S literature, and it applies to anyone under-eating relative to
  activity, vegan or not.
  Reference on the general mechanism: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4342163/
  (energy availability and menstrual status in exercising women)
- **Practical flag for the article:** a plant-based diet that is also low in
  total energy or heavily restricted (common when someone goes vegan and also
  cuts calories, or is an athlete with high output) carries the same
  amenorrhea risk as any other low-energy-availability diet. The risk is
  about energy adequacy, not about avoiding animal products, but the two
  often travel together in restrictive dieting, so it is fair to name the
  risk even while being precise about the mechanism.

## Gaps and open questions

- **No large, modern, well-controlled RCT tests a vegan/vegetarian diet
  specifically against dysmenorrhea pain.** Barnard 2000 (n=33) is still the
  most-cited primary source almost 25 years later, and it has not been
  replicated at scale or with blinding/weight-control for confounding.
- **The mechanistic chain (low fat → high SHBG → low estrogen → low
  prostaglandin → less pain) has never been demonstrated as a single
  connected pathway in one study.** Each link comes from a different, mostly
  small or older study, and the SHBG/estrogen link specifically has been
  contradicted by larger, more recent work (Soltani 2025 meta-analysis; Key
  et al. EPIC-Oxford-linked study).
- **No study directly measures menstrual prostaglandin levels before/after a
  dietary intervention in humans.** The diet-to-prostaglandin link is
  inferred from biochemistry (fatty acid precursor competition) and from
  omega-3 supplement trials measuring pain, not tissue-level prostaglandins.
  This should be written as hypothesis, not mechanism, in any article copy.
- **Fiber-and-estrogen evidence is genuinely split**, with older mechanistic
  studies supporting it and the best-measured recent study (Oh et al. 2015,
  NHS II) finding no strong association.
- **The most likely honest read of the whole picture:** weight and total
  energy intake, not "plant-based" status per se, may be doing most of the
  work in the studies that do show hormonal or symptom changes. Any article
  claim should distinguish "eating less fat/losing weight changed my
  hormones" from "eating plant-based changed my hormones," because the
  literature does not cleanly support the second, more specific claim.
- **Omega-3 evidence for dysmenorrhea pain is real but modest**, based on a
  meta-analysis with a wide confidence interval and small underlying trials,
  and it is evidence for supplementation, not for "plant-based eating"
  generally (a poorly planned plant-based diet can be omega-3 poor).
- **Iron risk is the most solid, most actionable downside** to name plainly.
  Cycle irregularity risk is real but is really an energy-availability risk
  that happens to co-occur with restrictive plant-based eating rather than a
  direct consequence of avoiding animal products.
