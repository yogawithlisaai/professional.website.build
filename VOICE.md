# VOICE.md

Reference for all writing on yogawithlisa.ai. Applies to blog posts, page copy,
and newsletter. Read this before drafting or editing any article.

Author: Lisa Eshun-Wilson, PhD. Molecular cell biologist, RYT-500 yoga instructor.

---

## Who I am writing for

Women in perimenopause, menopause, or burnout recovery. Many have been told their
symptoms are normal and have stopped expecting useful answers. Some are new to yoga
and unsure it is for them. A smaller group are college students.

Assume the reader is intelligent, tired, and short on time. She is not looking for
inspiration. She is looking for something that works and a reason to believe it.

## The position nobody else occupies

I am a molecular cell biologist who teaches yoga. That is the whole differentiator.
Every article should be one a Healthline writer could not have written and a wellness
influencer could not have written.

That means: when there is research, cite it and say what it actually found, including
the limits. When there is not, say so plainly. "We do not have good data on this yet"
is a sentence I am willing to write and most of my competitors are not.

## How I sound

Direct. Plain sentences. I would rather be clear than clever.

I explain mechanism, because knowing why something works makes people more likely to
do it. But I explain it the way I would to a smart friend, not the way I would in a
paper.

I am warm without being soft. I do not perform serenity. I am allowed to be funny,
dry, or a little blunt.

I disclose personal experience early, and I am specific about it. I was stiff. My
posture was bad. I had serious gut issues. I am the oldest of six. I found this
practice before I could afford it. That specificity is what makes the reader believe
I have been where she is.

I am willing to say something is unfair and mean it. When women are left to figure
out their own symptoms with no guidance, I say that is heartbreaking and unfair,
because it is. Controlled anger is part of my voice, not a lapse in it.

## Moves I actually make (see Examples below)

- **Acknowledge before advising.** Name what the reader said and validate it in one
  line before offering anything.
- **Build a concrete ladder.** Not "start small," but: take the stairs instead of the
  elevator, walk instead of ordering the Uber, then 10 minutes online, then a 30
  minute class in person. Specific rungs, in order.
- **Quote the felt thing.** Use quotation marks for subjective states the reader
  recognizes: a sense of "safety," "coming home to yourself." This is how I bridge
  mechanism and experience.
- **Name the constellation.** When symptoms are diffuse, list them: gut issues, mood
  swings, anxiety, sleep. Recognition is the point.
- **End with an invitation, not an instruction.** "I hope you can trust me to go on
  this journey with you" beats "start today."

## Structure

- Under a 5 minute read. Roughly 900 to 1,300 words.
- Open with the reader's actual problem, or with my own experience of it, in one or
  two sentences. No throat clearing, no "in today's fast paced world," no dictionary
  definitions.
- Short paragraphs. Two to four sentences.
- Subheads that say something, not labels. "Why the 4-7-8 pattern works" beats
  "Understanding Breathwork."
- End with one concrete thing she can do today. Not a summary of what she just read.

## Evidence rules

These are non-negotiable, because this is health content and being wrong here costs
more than being wrong elsewhere.

- Name the study or the finding. Link the source. Prefer primary literature and
  systematic reviews.
- Describe what the study actually measured. Sample size and population matter and
  I should say them when they undercut the claim.
- Distinguish "shown to" from "may" from "anecdotally." Never blur these.
- My own experience is an anecdote, always. It can open a post. It can never carry
  the causal claim. Write "this is what happened to me, and here is what the research
  says about why it might have," never "this worked for me, so it works."
- Never state a mechanism as established when it is a hypothesis.
- Never claim to treat, cure, diagnose, or prevent anything.
- When something is genuinely uncertain, say so. Uncertainty stated plainly builds
  more trust than false confidence.
- Be especially careful with HRT, supplements, and anything a reader might act on
  medically. Verify against current position statements, not against what I absorbed
  from the general conversation. The literature on HRT in particular has shifted
  substantially since 2002 and popular understanding has not kept up.
- If I would not defend a sentence to a reviewer, it does not go in.

### HRT: the corrected version

I got this wrong once in a draft, so it lives here now. Do not write that HRT causes
cancer and that nobody has challenged the claim. The opposite is true.

- The 2002 WHI results collapsed HRT use worldwide. Two decades of reanalysis
  followed, and the picture changed substantially.
- The Menopause Society 2022 position statement: benefits outweigh risks for most
  healthy symptomatic women under 60 and within 10 years of menopause onset. Risk
  stratification by age and time since menopause is the current standard.
- Breast cancer risk from hormone therapy is low in absolute terms: fewer than one
  additional case per 1,000 women per year. The panel compares that to two daily
  alcoholic drinks, obesity, or low physical activity.
- Risk does not increase appreciably with short-term estrogen-progestogen therapy,
  and may be decreased with estrogen alone.
- WHI findings in older women do not transfer to women with primary ovarian
  insufficiency or early menopause.

Source: https://menopause.org/wp-content/uploads/professional/nams-2022-hormone-therapy-position-statement.pdf

The real story, and the better one to write, is that a generation of women were
scared away from an effective treatment by findings that were later heavily
qualified, and most of them were never told. Check for anything more recent than
2022 before publishing, including FDA labeling changes.

## Never write

- Em dashes. Use a comma, a period, or a colon.
- "Unlock," "journey" as a standalone noun, "holistic," "sacred," "wellness journey,"
  "honor your body," "tune in," "hold space," "nourish," "radiant," "glow up."
- "Studies show" with no study named.
- "It's not just X, it's Y."
- Rhetorical question openers. "Ever feel like you're running on empty?"
- Em-dash asides, colon-then-reveal sentences, or scare quotes around invented terms.
  (Quotation marks around genuine felt states are fine. See Moves above.)
- Anything that implies the reader's symptoms are a mindset problem.
- Filler transitions: "that said," "at the end of the day," "the bottom line is."
- Exclamation points in body prose. They are fine in a direct greeting or a warm
  sign-off, where they sound like me.

## Formatting

- Sentence case for headings, not title case.
- Bold sparingly, for genuinely scannable takeaways only.
- Bullets when the content is a real list. Not to break up prose.
- Straight ASCII quotes and apostrophes. Not curly. (Site-wide convention.)
- Link out to sources inline rather than a reference list at the bottom.

## Author block

Ends every post. Two lines, no more:

> **Lisa Eshun-Wilson, PhD**
> Molecular cell biologist and 500-hour certified yoga instructor. [About](about.html)

No long credential paragraph. No "this article was medically reviewed by."

## Accessibility is the through-line

Free classes exist because cost is the reason most people never start. When an
article recommends something that costs money, name a free alternative if one exists.
Never assume the reader has a studio membership, a wearable, or a flexible schedule.

## Examples

These are unedited samples of how I actually write. Match this rhythm. When a draft
sounds unlike these, the draft is wrong, not the samples.

### Replying to a nervous new student

> Thank you so much for being so real and vulnerable with me. Can I start by saying
> you are not alone. For years, I was stiff, my posture was awful, and I was suffering
> from serious gut issues. I started with a few minutes a day of simple movements, or
> little opportunities throughout the day to change my habits, like taking the stairs
> instead of the elevator, or walking instead of taking that Uber. Ultimately I worked
> my way up to 10 or 15 minute classes online, and then full 30 or 45 minute in-person
> classes. Taking it step by step, one step at a time, with someone you can trust and
> be yourself with, will absolutely get you to where you want to be. I hope you can
> trust me to go on this journey with you.

What to notice: validation first. Personal disclosure with specifics, not
generalities. A concrete ladder with real rungs. Ends with an invitation.

### Explaining a mechanism

> When you exhale longer than you inhale, your nervous system shifts out of fight or
> flight and toward rest and digest. Vagal activity increases, which signals a sense
> of "safety" and lets you feel grounded in your body. This is what people are
> reaching for when they talk about "coming home to yourself."

What to notice: mechanism in plain words, then the felt experience in quotes. No
jargon that does not earn its place. Two to three sentences, not a paragraph.

### Naming what is unfair

> It can feel lonely and shameful. We are taught that aging is a disease, and most
> women have to discover and navigate their symptoms entirely on their own, which is
> heartbreaking and completely unfair. The experience is also confusing. The symptoms
> arrive as a constellation of seemingly unrelated problems: gut issues, mood swings,
> social anxiety, trouble sleeping. For some women it starts as early as 35.

What to notice: this is the strongest register I have. Direct, a little angry,
specific. The symptom list does real work because recognition is the point. Protect
this voice in any rewrite.
